# Letters across the world

In 1974 the United States marked the centenary of the Universal Postal Union with a set of eight stamps, each taking a detail from a painting or print of someone writing, reading or keeping letters. Constance Whitlock chose them for August, partly because they are beautiful and partly because they are good company on a summer evening.

The set is a small gallery. A woman reads, another writes, and a board of old letters and scraps hangs on a wall. The artists are Chardin, Terborch, Peto, Raphael, Hokusai, Liotard, Gainsborough and Goya, and Constance said that none of them ever thought about a postage stamp in their lives.

The second half of the evening was a reading: *Valentine's Day at the Post-Office*, from the first issue of *Household Words* in 1850, which Constance read in parts. Two men post three letters in Fleet Street and follow them through St. Martin's-le-Grand: the bag-openers, who are fined half a crown for every letter left in a sack; the stampers, who can mark three thousand an hour; and the "blind clerk", who can read any address at all. We recommend it for February.

The eighth stamp, Goya's portrait of Don Antonio Noriega, stayed in Constance's envelope, because she had lent her good copy to someone in 2019. Members were asked to write a letter before the next meeting and bring the envelope. Nine did. One came back undelivered, which pleased Teddy Marchetti more than anything else that evening.
