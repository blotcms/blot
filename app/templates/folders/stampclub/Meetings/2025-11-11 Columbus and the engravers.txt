# Columbus and the engravers

Walter Dziedzic spent thirty-four years writing railroad timetables, and he says he chose Columbus because Columbus also arrived somewhere other than where he was going. He brought two issues, a quarter of a century apart, that are often muddled.

The first is the 15c Landing of Columbus of 1869, one of the pictorial stamps that were the first American issues to show a scene instead of a portrait, and that were mocked for it and replaced within a year. The frame is printed in one color and the picture in another, in separate passes, so the picture can sit a little off-center in its frame. Walter showed us one where the boats ride a little low and said, *this is not an error, this is a mood*.

![15c Landing of Columbus, 1869, with a G grill](/_stamps/15c Landing of Columbus G Grill single.jpg)

The second is the Columbian issue of 1893, sixteen stamps from one cent to five dollars, printed for the World's Columbian Exposition in Chicago, each in a single color. It tells the voyage in order, more or less, and collectors have been arguing about the more or less since.

![4c Fleet of Columbus, 1893](/_stamps/4c Fleet of Columbus single.jpg)
![4c Fleet of Columbus in ultramarine, 1893](/_stamps/4c ultramarine Fleet of Columbus single.jpg)
![5c Columbus Soliciting Aid from Queen Isabella, 1893](/_stamps/5c Columbus Soliciting Aid from Queen Isabella single.jpg)

The two 4c stamps, he said, are the classic confusion: one is ultramarine and the other a plainer blue, and the difference shows only in good daylight, so he held the window open for the room.

He closed by reading *The Great British Gum Secret*, from *Household Words* in 1852, which traces the gum on the back of the penny stamp to a starch works near Dublin that caught fire in 1821, and disposes of the rumor that it was made of fish. The full text is in [Readings](/tagged/readings). A fine thing, Walter said, to know before one licks anything.
