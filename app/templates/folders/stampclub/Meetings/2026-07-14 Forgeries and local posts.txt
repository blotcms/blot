# Forgeries and local posts

Before the federal government ran the mail in cities, anyone with a horse and an idea could. Leonard Frisch, who has set type for forty years, gave us the evening's longest talk and its most careful one.

## The private posts

In the 1840s several firms ran letter carriage across New York for a cent or two, under names such as City Dispatch and Letter Mail. Their stamps were simple, and many were printed from whatever plates the printer had to hand. Leonard says that is where the trouble starts: if a stamp is easy to print, it is easy to copy.

## How to look at one

Leonard's method is the typesetter's: do not look at the picture, look at the letters. A forger copies the design, but the spacing between the letters is the part nobody thinks to check. He showed us three tells:

- uneven gaps in the lettering
- a border that is slightly too regular
- ink that sits on the paper instead of in it

He also pointed out that some forgeries are worth more than the originals, because they were made in smaller numbers and sold as curiosities in their own day. This led to a long argument that was not resolved, and a shorter one about lunch.

![30c Eagle from the Great Central Sanitary Fair](/_stamps/30c Eagle Great Central Sanitary Fair single.jpg)

The last stamp he showed is not a forgery at all, but a charity label from the Civil War, printed for a fair. He included it, he said, because it is beautiful.
