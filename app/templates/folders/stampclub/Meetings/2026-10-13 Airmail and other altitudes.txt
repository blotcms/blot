# Airmail and other altitudes

Dr. Harold Pruitt has collected nothing but airmail for thirty-one years. He says this is not a limitation but a discipline, and that other members are welcome to try it as soon as they have recovered from the shock of the prices.

He began with the most famous error in American philately: the 24-cent Curtiss Jenny of 1918, in which the biplane was printed upside down. Fewer than a hundred were ever found. Dr. Pruitt holds that the man who bought the sheet for a dollar's worth of face value did not know what he had, and that the man at the post office window who handed it over, after first offering him a sheet with the plane the right way up, did not know either. He also holds that this is the best story in the hobby and has not been persuaded otherwise.

The second half was about speed. Dr. Pruitt traced how mail went from rail to air and then, he said sadly, to a van with a plane's name on it. He brought covers carried on first flights, and a rule of thumb: the more the cachet apologizes for the weather, the better the cover.

Questions ran over by twenty minutes. Marguerite Fong asked whether a mailgram counts as airmail. Dr. Pruitt asked her to see him afterwards.
