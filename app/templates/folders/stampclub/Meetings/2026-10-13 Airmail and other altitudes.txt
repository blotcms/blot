# Airmail and other altitudes

Dr. Harold Pruitt has collected nothing but airmail since he joined the club in 1994. He says this is not a limitation but a discipline, and that other members are welcome to try it as soon as they have recovered from the prices.

He began with the most famous error in American stamps: the 24-cent Curtiss Jenny of 1918, on which the biplane was printed upside down. Exactly one sheet of a hundred was ever found. A collector bought it across a post office counter in Washington for its face value, twenty-four dollars, and knew precisely what he had. Dr. Pruitt holds that the clerk who sold it to him did not, and that this is the best story in the hobby. He has not been persuaded otherwise.

The second half was about speed. Dr. Pruitt traced how mail went from rail to air and then, he said sadly, to a van with a plane painted on the side. He brought covers carried on first flights, and a rule of thumb: the more the cachet apologizes for the weather, the better the cover.

Questions ran over by twenty minutes, and the librarian let us. Marguerite Fong asked whether a mailgram counts as airmail. Dr. Pruitt asked her to see him afterwards.
