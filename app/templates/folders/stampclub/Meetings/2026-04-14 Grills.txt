# Grills

Priya Venkataraman keeps the club's accounts and, in her spare time, a drawer of grilled stamps. She says the two interests are related, because both are about stopping people from using the same thing twice.

A grill is a pattern of tiny pyramids pressed into the paper of a stamp, so that the cancelling ink soaks in and cannot be washed off.[^1] The Post Office introduced it in 1867 and gave it up within a few years, partly because the grills were a nuisance to make and partly because nobody could show that washed stamps were ever a problem worth the trouble.

She passed round a stamp under a magnifying glass, with the grill visible as a faint crosshatch. Her tip is to tilt it toward the light until the pattern appears. With good eyes and patience, you can see it from a foot away.

The last stamp was the trick question: a re-issue of 1875, printed on hard white paper with no grill at all, which is one way to tell it from the original.[^2] Priya then reported on the club's finances. They are in order, and the tea fund is not.

[^1]: The grills were sorted by collectors into types by their size and shape, and named by letter, which tells you which one you have.
[^2]: Only one way. Priya says every collector should own a loupe and a patient friend.
